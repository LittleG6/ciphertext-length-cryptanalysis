An investigator entered the quiet library shortly after sunrise and placed a folder beside the reading lamp. The folder contained a collection of ordinary messages transformed by unknown substitution keys. Each message preserved its spaces, but every letter had been exchanged according to a fixed secret alphabet. The task was not merely to find one attractive interpretation. The investigator wanted to measure how often the genuine message appeared near the top of a ranked list.

For every trial, a passage was selected from material that had never been used to train the scoring model. A new random key converted the passage into ciphertext. The search procedure then began from several different guesses. At each step it swapped two letter assignments, decoded the message, and compared the resulting language score with the current score. Promising changes were retained, and distinct candidates were stored in a bounded archive.

The shortest messages offered little evidence. Some contained no rare letters and only a handful of repeated combinations. Incorrect mappings could create fragments that looked surprisingly reasonable. As the messages became longer, regular word shapes and recurring character sequences placed more pressure on the key. The correct interpretation was expected to move upward in the ranking, although the search could still settle in an unhelpful region of the enormous permutation space.

After the run, the experiment recorded whether the exact plaintext had been generated. If it was present, its rank among the retained candidates was measured. If it was absent, the result was treated as larger than the archive limit rather than assigned an invented exact rank. The known plaintext was also scored separately and inserted only for diagnosis. This second rank showed whether the scoring model would have recognized the answer if the search had supplied it.

The final report compared recovery curves across message lengths. It included confidence intervals, reciprocal rank, character accuracy, observed key accuracy, runtime, and the gap between the true score and the best incorrect score. The report also separated first place success, lower ranked generation, and failure to generate the answer. This distinction made the conclusions more useful because improvements to search and improvements to language scoring address different weaknesses.

No single experiment can establish a universal minimum ciphertext length. Results depend on the source of the language, the treatment of spaces, the order and smoothing of the character model, and the amount of computation. The purpose of the study was therefore narrower and more defensible: to estimate length effects under a clearly stated protocol and to provide code that another researcher could rerun, inspect, and modify.
